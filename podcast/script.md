## Introduction {#intro}

Hey, and welcome to the Forever Class Codex.

If you're listening to this, you've probably already spent more hours in Azeroth than you'd like to admit. You remember when Paladins couldn't taunt. You remember Hunters arguing about the dead zone, and Druids powershifting with a Wolfshead Helm. So I'm not going to explain what a Warrior is. Instead, we're going to walk through what World of Warcraft Forever actually changes about each of the nine Alliance classes, and which of those changes you'll really feel.

A quick note on timing. I'm recording this on October twenty-second, twenty twenty-six. Forever launches on November fourth. The last beta build that touched classes was the October sixteenth build, and everything you'll hear includes those late changes. But it's still beta, and Blizzard has been clear that talents and abilities can keep moving right up to launch. So take this as a snapshot, not scripture.

Before we get into the classes, it helps to understand what Forever is trying to be. And maybe more importantly, what it isn't.

It isn't Vanilla with every spec swapped out for its modern Retail version. The pace is still Classic. A normal outdoor mob is still supposed to take you ten to fifteen seconds. Threat still matters. Crowd control still matters. You'll still be watching your mana bar, your swing timer, and where you're standing.

What Blizzard has done is go after the places where the original classes were unfinished. Specs that never really did the job they advertised. Resource systems that fought you. Talents you had to buy just to make the class function. Almost everything new in Forever is aimed at one of those problems. So as we go, keep that question in mind. Not, what's new? But, what was broken, and did they fix it?

## System changes {#systems}

Let's start with the changes that touch everyone, because they color everything that follows.

The talent trees are still Classic trees. Rows unlock as you spend points, and there's still a thirty-one point capstone at the bottom. But there's now a new milestone at sixteen points, sitting between the familiar eleven and twenty-one. Blizzard calls it the gold medal tier, and a lot of the most interesting talents in the game live there. At the same time, a bunch of talents that everyone was forced to buy are now just baseline. Divine Spirit. Blessing of Kings. The improved Mark of the Wild style effects. The result is that your talent points go toward actual decisions, instead of buying back basic class function.

The second big one is dual specialization at level forty. If you ever played a hybrid in Vanilla, you know how much that matters. No more paying a respec fee every time you want to switch from healing dungeons to questing on your own. If you like two specs of the same class, that's now a perfectly good reason to roll it.

Third, hit and crit have been merged. There's no longer a melee version and a spell version of each stat. Caster weapons carry real spell power. And here's a nice touch for healers: healing power now counts as one third damage. So your healing gear no longer leaves you miserable when you're out in the world on your own.

Fourth, the old downranking trick is mostly gone. Spells far below your level get much less benefit from your spell power, and lower chances to proc things. Blizzard's own example is the level sixty Mage spamming rank one Frostbolt, which no longer gets the normal Frostbite effect. When the old trick did something genuinely useful, Blizzard has tended to add a real spell to replace it, and we'll hear one of those when we get to Mage.

And fifth, raid buffs care much less about who's in your group. Late in beta, Battle Shout became raid-wide. So did Trueshot Aura, Moonkin Aura and Leader of the Pack. So did the big Shaman totems: Grace of Air, Strength of Earth, Windfury and Flametongue. Paladin Salvation no longer takes up one of your Blessing slots either. That old ritual of building five-person groups around who buffs whom is mostly a thing of the past.

There's also some Alliance-specific news. Dwarves can now be Shamans, which we'll come back to. Humans can be Hunters, and Gnomes can be Priests. And the new Skyborne race can be a Warrior, Hunter, Rogue, Druid or Mage. Finally, the weapon skill racials are gone for good. After Blizzard's last pass before launch, Human Sword Specialization is a flat one percent crit, called Ruthless, alongside a bigger ten percent Spirit bonus. And Dwarves now get one percent hit, called Artisan's Precision. So the old logic, that Human is the melee race because of swords, doesn't really hold anymore.

Alright. With that foundation in place, let's go class by class. For each one, I'll tell you roughly how far it's moved from Vanilla, what's changed, and who it's for.

## Warrior {#warrior}

We'll start with the Warrior, because it's the best example of a class Blizzard deliberately didn't reinvent.

Of all nine classes, Warrior has moved the least. If Vanilla Warrior is already exactly what you love, Forever won't take that away from you. What it does instead is go after the places where the class was fighting its own mechanics.

The biggest of those is rage. In Vanilla, rage from getting hit was calculated after your armor and absorbs, which meant better gear, or a nice Priest shield, actually starved you of resource. In Forever, that rage is calculated before mitigation. So gearing up and getting shielded no longer punishes you. Offensive rage is also tied much more closely to weapon speed, and critical hits give you a little extra. Rage becomes more predictable. You still have to manage it, you just aren't fighting it.

Stance dancing gets easier too. Tactical Mastery is now baseline at level fourteen, so you keep ten rage when you switch stances without spending a single talent point. And at level twenty you learn Victory Rush, which heals you for ten percent of your health after a kill. It's a small thing, but anyone who leveled a Warrior knows that the class used to grind to a halt the moment your health got low. Now each kill helps carry you into the next pull.

The big cooldowns are untangled as well. Recklessness, Retaliation and Shield Wall no longer share a cooldown, and Retaliation and Shield Wall come down to fifteen minutes. Rend and Thunder Clap scale with your attack power, Thunder Clap works in Defensive Stance, and Sunder Armor's threat scales with attack power too.

Now the specs. Arms gets a talent called Weaponmaster, which folds the old sword, axe and mace specializations together. Axes and polearms lean toward crit, maces and staves give armor penetration, and swords keep their extra attacks. So you're no longer stuck in a talent build that doesn't match the weapon that just dropped. Around that, Arms gets Spearing Strike at sixteen points, a talent called Bloodthrill that lets Rend set up Overpower, and an Improved Slam that no longer resets your auto attack. Arms stops being a wait for Mortal Strike, and becomes a weapon master spec built around Overpower, Rend and Slam.

Fury's new sixteen point talent is Raging Blows, which makes Whirlwind work better with two weapons and makes Cleave cheaper. Off-hand hit is less painful, Enrage can trigger when you take damage, and Death Wish now costs you extra damage taken instead of armor. And Bloodthirst loses its tiny heal in exchange for a burst of movement speed.

Protection is still classic sword and board, just with better plumbing. You get more rage from blocks. A talent called Master of Defense gives rage when your shield helps you avoid an attack. Vanguard lets you Charge straight into Defensive Stance play. And Improved Shield Block and Improved Taunt are moving toward baseline.

So who is Warrior for? It's for the player who specifically doesn't want Forever to feel like a different expansion. You're still living on rage, stances, weapon swings and threat. It's just that the class no longer gets in its own way.

## Paladin {#paladin}

If Warrior is the class Blizzard barely touched, Paladin is the opposite. This is one of the biggest overhauls in the entire game.

Blizzard's goal is stated pretty plainly. A Paladin should be able to be a real healer, a real tank, or a real Retribution damage dealer. Anyone who tanked on a Vanilla Paladin, or tried to make Ret work in a raid, knows that wasn't true the first time around.

Two baseline changes set the tone. Every Paladin now learns Holy Strike early on, an instant Holy damage weapon attack. And Judgment no longer eats your Seal. That one change gets rid of the old judge, reseal, judge, reseal routine that made Vanilla Paladin feel so passive.

Consecration is now baseline at level twenty instead of being a Holy talent. Only the first four enemies take its full damage and threat, so it isn't an unlimited area bomb. In fact, late beta cut its spell power scaling significantly, while raising Righteous Fury's Holy threat from sixty percent to ninety. Blizzard is clearly steering Protection toward deliberate tank threat, rather than one giant scaling spell.

And if you loved Reckoning bombs, here's some good news. Reckoning keeps its Classic behavior. It stores up to four charges and unleashes them on your next auto attack.

Now, Protection. The heart of the new Prot Paladin is Seal of Fury. It adds Holy damage to your attacks, it favors fast weapons, and while you have a shield equipped it turns some of that into absorb shields. And here's the part that matters most. Judging Seal of Fury taunts. The Alliance Paladin finally has a real taunt.

The talents build around that seal. Its mitigation returns mana to you. Swift Judgment brings Judgment back faster. Iron Creed ties Holy Strike to your defenses. And Templar's Bulwark is a big absorb cooldown that scales with your maximum health. Put all of that together, and Prot Paladin has a tank seal, a taunt, baseline Consecration, much stronger threat, real mitigation, mana sustain and a proper emergency button. It's no longer a class that can tank five-mans as long as everyone works around it.

Retribution gets much more to do, too. Its sixteen point talent, Sacred Arbiter, empowers Holy Strike and refreshes your Judgments. Champion of the Light converts some of your Intellect into spell damage, although late beta cut that from twenty, forty and sixty percent, down to ten, twenty and thirty. Vindication now lowers the enemy's attack power and raises yours at the same time. And then there's my favorite, Twist of Light. Seal twisting used to be an accident of the swing timer, something you learned from a forum guide. In Forever it's a talent. Your previous seal echoes into your next melee hit. Ret keeps seal twisting, but now it's part of the design.

Holy changes its role the least, but it gets a much richer spell rotation. Voice of Truth helps you resist being silenced or interrupted. Reverence improves your mana regeneration in combat. Infusion of Light means a critical Holy Shock or Flash of Light speeds up your next Holy Light. Divine Favor sits at sixteen points, and Holy Shock is deeper in the tree with a much shorter cooldown than you remember. The capstone, Light's Vigil, resets Holy Shock and then pays off with either damage or a party heal, depending on what you target. Instead of carefully rank-picking Flash of Light all night, Holy now runs on a Holy Shock engine.

So here's the takeaway. If you liked the idea of a Vanilla Paladin, but not what actually happened when you tried to tank, or play Ret at sixty, this is the clearest play-it-again pick in Forever. It's still slow, it still drinks mana, it's still seals and judgments. It just finally has enough machinery in all three specs.

## Hunter {#hunter}

Next up, the Hunter. And I think the Hunter has changed more than people expect, because the ranged rotation looks familiar on the surface.

The real story is that the three trees are now three different ways to play. In Vanilla, the specs were mostly different ways to add damage to the same pet and the same bow. In Forever, they each have their own identity.

First, some baseline changes. Every Hunter learns Aimed Shot at level twenty. Arcane Shot no longer shares a cooldown with it. Multi-Shot does instead. Traps can be used in combat, and fire and ice traps have separate cooldowns, so you can keep a Fire Trap and an Ice Trap down at the same time. That changes a lot, especially in dungeons. Traps stop being something you only set up before a pull, or after a Feign Death, and become part of the fight.

Pets get one of the deepest redesigns in the game. They now inherit their stats from your gear. Every family gets the basics, an attack like Bite or Claw, and a movement ability like Dash or Dive. And each family also gets its own utility ability. Blizzard has been trimming the strongest of those, though. In the October sixteenth build, the Crocolisk's healing reduction was cut from fifty percent to twenty-five, the Bat's casting slow was halved, and the Bird of Prey was toned down too. So which pet you tame matters more than it did in Vanilla, but don't assume the early beta pets will survive to launch untouched.

Now the specs. Beast Mastery gets more active, with Focused Fire and, at sixteen points, Summon Hawk, which brings in a temporary second attacker on your target. It isn't the modern zoo, but there's more going on than your pet's auto attacks getting bigger.

Marksmanship finally gives you a real petless option. A talent called Lone Wolf gives you a big damage bonus when you play without a pet. Trueshot Aura moves deeper into the tree and becomes raid-wide. And the capstone is Sniper Shot, a long cast, extreme range precision shot. If you only ever kept a pet because Vanilla forced you to, Marksmanship is now built for you.

And then there's Survival. Survival is the dramatic one. It's no longer a collection of PvP and control talents. It's now a deliberate melee and ranged hybrid. Predator's Edge boosts your melee crit damage and off-hand. Expose Prey lets attacks on your marked target set up Mongoose Bite. Strider Kick is a weapon attack tied to movement. And Lacerating Strikes turns repeated Mongoose Bites into a bleed. Importantly, the Classic dead zone is still there. Survival isn't about erasing range. It's about moving through those ranges on purpose, with something useful to do on both sides.

One last detail. There's now an option to switch automatically between your melee auto attack and Auto Shot. Auto Shot still needs you to stand still for half a second, but it can fire while you're casting Aimed Shot or Sniper Shot. Shot timing is still part of the class.

So, who's it for? If you want a Hunter that still plays by old school Hunter rules, but with three genuinely different fantasies, Forever Hunter is one of the most exciting redesigns. And Survival, especially, is almost a brand new spec.

## Rogue {#rogue}

From the Hunter's big shake-up, we come to the Rogue, which is much closer to the Warrior's philosophy.

Blizzard clearly thinks the core of the Vanilla Rogue is already good. Energy, combo points, positional attacks, poisons, stealth, control. All of that stays. Most of the changes are about making the three trees clearer.

The baseline quality of life is still nice. Poisons carry more charges, and they now stack with sharpening stones and weightstones. Sap no longer pulls you out of stealth. Distract also lowers the enemy's ability to spot stealth. Expose Armor is now worth exactly five Sunders, and it no longer stops Warriors from using Sunder Armor, which ends a very old raid argument. And Rogues can use one-handed axes now.

Assassination changes the most. It becomes the dagger and poison spec, explicitly. Mutilate is the twenty-one point centerpiece. It strikes with both daggers, does more to poisoned targets, and awards two combo points. The capstone is Venom, a finisher that boosts your poisons. So in practice, Assassination is about keeping Slice and Dice and Venom running, with your poisons as the real engine of your damage.

Combat stays the most recognizably Classic tree. A talent called Hack and Slash merges the old weapon specializations, so you're rarely holding the wrong weapon type for your build. Precision applies more broadly, Backstab gets more support, and Flawless Execution makes Eviscerate cheaper, so your finishers flow better.

Subtlety is still the stealth, setup and control tree, but it gets a clearer damage loop. Premeditation sits at sixteen points. Hemorrhage now boosts your own Rupture, instead of mainly being a debuff for the rest of the raid. Cutthroat lets Backstab open up Ambush-like attacks in combat. And the capstone, Thousand Cuts, lets your Rupture ticks reduce the energy cost of Hemorrhage and Backstab.

Blizzard has also said they want Rogue to have the highest, or near the highest, single-target damage in a straightforward stand-and-fight encounter. The trade-off is less utility and no range. But remember, that's a design goal, not a promise about launch balance. I wouldn't roll a Rogue just because it's supposed to top the meters.

So, the Rogue is for someone who already loves the Classic Rogue and wants the trees to make more sense. It's not the class to pick if you're looking for something radically new.

## Priest {#priest}

The Priest is where things start to feel a bit more like later expansions, while still keeping that Classic mana management and casting pace. And all three specs come out of it much more complete.

The headline change is that Fear Ward and Devouring Plague are no longer locked to particular races. Every Priest gets both, and every race still gets two racial Priest spells of its own. So if you're Alliance, Humans keep options like Feedback, Dwarves keep Desperate Prayer, Night Elves keep Starshards and Elune's Grace, and the new Gnome Priests get their own spells. Race still matters for flavor, but nobody needs you to be a Dwarf for Fear Ward anymore.

Divine Spirit and Shadow Word: Death are both baseline. Prayer of Healing can target another party. Several Priests can keep Renew on the same target. And Fade drops more threat. Late beta also adjusted the shadow spells. Devouring Plague's cooldown came down from a minute to twenty-four seconds, and Shadow Word: Death's went up from twelve seconds to eighteen.

Now, if there's one spec in the whole game where you'll need to relearn what it's for, it's Discipline. In Vanilla, Disc was the tree you took Power Infusion and Inner Focus from, along with some mana efficiency and a few shield talents. In Forever, it's a real healing style, built on shields and on weaving damage into your healing.

Penance is the twenty-one point spell, and it can either damage an enemy or heal a friend. Soul Warding makes repeated shields better. Renewed Hope keys off Weakened Soul. And Divine Aegis turns your critical direct heals into absorb shields. Then there's Power in Light, which links Smite and Penance to targets that have Holy Fire or Shadow Word: Pain on them. That's the key. It gives you a reason to weave damage into your healing on purpose, not just when the group happens to be topped off.

Holy, meanwhile, gets Binding Heal at sixteen points, and Prayer of Mending as its capstone, bouncing from ally to ally the way you remember from later expansions. There's also Litany of Light, which rewards you for switching between different heals instead of casting the same one over and over. And Lightwell is gone. In Vanilla, Holy had dozens of spell ranks but everyone settled on the same efficient few. Forever actively rewards you for using the whole kit.

Shadow fixes some famous Vanilla problems, without turning into modern Shadow. Shadowform lets you cast more of your non-healing spells, and adds a big mana efficiency and crit damage boost. Shadow Weaving is now a personal buff, so you're much less likely to be invited just as the Warlocks' debuff Priest. Vampiric Embrace becomes an activated thirty second cooldown. Devouring Contagion spreads Devouring Plague to two more targets. Early Demise turns Shadow Word: Death into an execute. And there's a lovely new talent called Dark Inspiration, where Mind Blast and fully channeled Mind Flays raise Spirit for your whole party. Combined with Mind Flay ticks that ramp up as the channel goes on, your filler spell finally has a reason to finish instead of being clipped.

So the Priest is for you if you want to stay recognizably Classic, but feel like your spec matters every time you log in. And if you've never enjoyed Disc before, it might be the sleeper pick of the whole expansion.

## Shaman {#shaman}

And now, the Shaman. For an Alliance player, this is the most novel choice in the game by definition, because you simply couldn't roll one in Vanilla. Forever adds Dwarf Shamans to the Alliance. And even if you've played a Horde Shaman to death, this version has one of the longest quality of life lists of any class.

Let's start with the totems. Totems now use a one second global cooldown instead of one and a half. Long buff totems last five minutes instead of two. There's a dedicated totem interface. Fire Nova is now a spell you trigger through your fire totem, instead of a separate totem you throw away. And there's a whole set of tools for managing them. Totemic Recall pulls your totems back for some mana. Totemic Projection moves the totems you already have down. And three presets, Call of the Elements, Call of the Spirits and Call of the Ancestors, drop a whole set of totems at once. All of that together removes most of the old four-button totem piano. You still think about your totems. You just stop spending half your globals rebuilding the same four.

The late beta made the buff totems friendlier too. Grace of Air can now run alongside Windfury or Flametongue, though Windfury and Flametongue still can't be used together. And all four, Grace of Air, Strength of Earth, Windfury and Flametongue, are raid-wide. Chain Lightning also got about thirty-five percent cheaper.

Elemental gets a real casting rotation. Lightning Bolt and Chain Lightning cast faster than they used to. And here's a place where the latest build really matters. An earlier version of the beta had removed Elemental Mastery entirely, and put Lava Burst deep in the tree. The October sixteenth build reversed that. Lava Burst moved up to twenty-one points, and Elemental Mastery came back as the capstone. It's a three minute cooldown that, for fifteen seconds, speeds up your Lightning Bolt and Lava Burst casts and removes Chain Lightning's cooldown. So Elemental now has a real rhythm, Flame Shock, Lava Burst, Lightning Bolt and Chain Lightning, with a burst window on top.

Enhancement might be even more interesting. Mental Dexterity and Mental Quickness turn your Intellect into attack power and spell power. Stormstrike moves all the way up to sixteen points, on an eight second cooldown, and its debuff powers up your next Lightning Bolt, Chain Lightning or Earth Shock. Then Maelstrom Weapon speeds up those spells as you fight, eventually making them instant. You still swing a big two-handed axe or mace. This isn't Retail's dual wield Enhancement. But your melee and your spells finally work together, instead of just sitting next to each other. There's also some tanking support, but Blizzard frames it as off-tanking or small group tanking, not a fourth raid tank.

Restoration gets the tools to break out of Vanilla's one-note Chain Heal identity. Water Shield restores mana, and it can now trigger off any critical spell, not just critical heals. Mana Tide moves up to sixteen points, with an extra rank at level thirty-eight. And the capstone is Riptide, an instant heal with a heal over time that also strengthens Chain Heal on that target. Resto becomes about setting up your Chain Heals and juggling several spells, not just spamming one.

So, if you're an Alliance veteran looking for something that still feels like old World of Warcraft, but that you literally couldn't play on the Alliance before, Dwarf Shaman belongs very high on your list.

## Mage {#mage}

Let's move on to the Mage. If the Shaman is a whole new class for the Alliance, the Mage is a familiar one with new engines bolted on.

The core is the same. Blink, Polymorph, Counterspell, Frost Nova, mana management. But each spec now has its own way of playing at the top end.

A couple of baseline changes first. At level forty you learn Frostfire Bolt, which counts as both Fire and Frost and takes whichever is better for resistances and talents. It exists to make Fire and Frost hybrid builds legitimate.

At the same time, Blizzard is reining in some of the Mage's most degenerate farming tricks. Improved Blizzard slows less than it did. Cone of Cold's control was toned down, although the October build restored its full effect on the first four targets. So Mages can still handle small packs well, they just won't dominate enormous mob trains forever. And because rank one Frostbolt is penalized now, there's a new spell at level twenty-four called Frost Blast. It's a quick Frost cast with a forty percent slow, and it benefits from your Frostbolt talents. It keeps the useful thing rank one Frostbolt did, and closes the loophole.

Now, Arcane. Arcane is almost unrecognizable. It's a real damage spec now. Arcane Blast is a one and a half second cast. Each one gives you spell damage equal to fifteen percent of your Intellect, stacking up to four times, but each one also makes the next Arcane Blast more expensive. The stacks last fifteen seconds. So the whole spec becomes a very Classic puzzle. How far do you push your spell power before the mana cost becomes a problem? Missile Barrage can now proc from Arcane Missiles itself, and it moved much earlier in the tree. And at sixteen points there's Arcane Wrath, a long cast whose damage scales partly with your maximum mana. Your enormous mana pool stops being just a fuel tank, and becomes part of your damage.

Fire becomes much more about procs. Heating Up builds toward faster Pyroblasts. Every Fire Blast adds a charge, and a critical Fire Blast adds two. Critical Mass now gives ten percent crit at five points. Fire Power adds damage for each of your own Fire effects on the target, up to twelve percent. And Ignite is now tracked per Mage, which ends the old shared Ignite mess. Instead of spamming Fireball and hoping, you load up a target with your fire effects and turn your crits into Pyroblasts.

Frost gets the most familiar imports from later expansions, Ice Lance and Fingers of Frost. Fingers of Frost lets your attacks treat a target as frozen, and Ice Lance hits much harder on frozen targets, though late beta cut that bonus from three hundred percent down to two hundred. And Winter's Chill is completely reworked. It's no longer a stacking crit debuff. Frostbolt, Frost Blast and empowered Ice Lances now build up stacks, and your next Cone of Cold cashes them in for big damage. Cone of Cold stops being just an emergency slow, and becomes the finisher of a combo.

So, if your complaint about Vanilla Mage was that the toolkit is amazing, but raiding turns into pressing one button, Forever's answer isn't Retail Mage. It's Classic Mage, with a real engine for each spec. And Arcane especially feels like a brand new specialization.

## Warlock {#warlock}

From one caster to another. The Warlock is a class that didn't need rescuing in Vanilla. It already worked. So Forever's changes are less about fixing it and more about making its three specs feel truly distinct. And I'd argue it's the cleanest three-way split among the pure casters.

There's one deceptively big baseline change. Curse of Agony and Curse of Doom become Banes. Banes are their own category, separate from your utility curses, so putting your damage over time on a target no longer means giving up the group's utility curse. Curse of the Elements now covers more magic schools, and Curse of Shadow has been folded away.

Firestones and Spellstones become weapon enchantments that stack with oils. Life Tap now scales with your Spirit, tying your gear directly to your health-to-mana engine. Drain Soul gives you soul shards more reliably. And your demons inherit more of your stats.

Affliction becomes a true damage over time and drain spec. Pandemic lets your dots and drains critically strike. Soul Siphon rewards you for stacking more of your Affliction effects on a target. And the capstone, Wrack, is a channeled shadow spell that amplifies the dots already ticking. Late beta also doubled Shadow Mastery, so it now goes up to ten percent shadow damage, and added a talent called Grim Reach to extend your range. For the first time, deep Affliction has a reason to stay deep Affliction at endgame.

Demonology does something really clever. Demonic Sacrifice moves much earlier in the tree. Then the capstone, Demonic Pact, lets you keep the bonus from sacrificing one demon, while summoning and keeping another. It resolves the old joke, that one of the most famous Demonology builds was killing your demon for a buff. Add Decimation, which turns Soul Fire into an execute, and Demonic Brand, which improves your pet's threat and damage, and Demonology is finally committed to the demon.

Destruction gets a stronger mix of fire and shadow. Conflagrate arrives much earlier, at sixteen points. Your shadow and fire spells now strengthen each other through new talents. Bane of Havoc lets you mark a second target, which takes some of the damage you deal elsewhere. And the capstone is Incinerate, which hits much harder on targets with Immolate, giving fire Destruction a real filler spell.

So the Warlock is for you if you love the Classic Warlock toolbox, but always wished the endgame builds felt like three real specs, instead of all blurring together.

## Druid {#druid}

And finally, the Druid. I saved it for last because I think it might be the deepest redesign in the whole game. The changes go all the way down to how the class works at an engine level.

The most important one is this. When you're shapeshifted, your melee damage now scales with your weapon's damage. Forms still have fixed attack speeds, but a better weapon means better Cat and Bear damage. That ends the Vanilla assumption that a Feral Druid's weapon is just a bag of stats, and that odd items like the Manual Crowd Pummeler define the spec.

Forms are friendlier in general, too. You can use more objects, consumables and racials while shifted. Barkskin works in forms. Entangling Roots works indoors. Nature's Grasp is baseline early. Omen of Clarity is baseline at level twenty. And, at last, Druids get a normal out of combat resurrection, called Revive. Bear form gets Lacerate at level forty-two, for stacking bleed and threat, and Swipe scales with attack power. You're immune to Disarm once you're shifted, although a Disarm before you shift still hurts. And as of the October sixteenth build, Furor lets you keep your Cat energy when you briefly shift through Bear, although your energy won't regenerate while you're a Bear.

Balance gets a new Eclipse mechanic, but not the modern one with a bar that swings back and forth by itself. In Forever, casting Wrath shortens your next Starfires, which gives you a reason to alternate between the two. Insect Swarm sits at sixteen points. Moonkin Form lets you cast more of your non-healing spells. And Moonkin Aura is now raid-wide spell crit. Balance is no longer a Starfire caster with chronic mana problems and a novelty aura. But it's still slow casting, Classic Balance.

Feral is the change that will fascinate anyone who remembers powershifting. Tiger's Fury is gone, and in its place is Shifting Power, which converts a chunk of your mana into energy on a cooldown. Think about what that means. Mana is still a secondary resource you spend to fuel your Cat damage, just like powershifting. But now it's a deliberate talent, instead of canceling and re-entering Cat Form over and over in a Wolfshead Helm. On top of that, Claw, Shred and Rake scale better, and Primal Bite becomes a key attack. And Berserk, deep in the tree, removes Primal Bite's cooldown, makes it hit several enemies, and greatly boosts the crit chance of your combo point builders. Blizzard took the core idea behind powershifting, and turned it into real design.

Restoration gets some of the most recognizable imports. Swiftmend moves to sixteen points, and it no longer eats the heal over time it uses. That alone is a huge quality of life win. The capstone is Wild Growth, healing several party members over time. And Gift of the Earthmother speeds up Rejuvenation, Swiftmend and Wild Growth. Resto keeps its Classic heals over time and Healing Touch identity, but now it has a real instant reactive heal and a proper group heal.

So, if you know Classic deeply, Druid might be the class where you notice the changes the most. Weapon upgrades matter in forms. Powershifting is formalized. Bears have better threat tools. Balance has a real spell cycle. And Resto gets the core healing tools it was always missing. It's still unmistakably a Vanilla Druid, just without a remarkable number of its old oddities.

## Choosing a class {#choose}

So that's all nine. Let's pull it together. How should a veteran actually choose?

If you want the most dramatically improved version of a class you already know, look at Paladin, Druid, Shaman, Hunter, Priest and Mage. In those six, the changes touch what you do often enough that you'll keep catching yourself thinking, oh, this is Forever.

If what you really want is Vanilla, with the rough edges sanded off, then Warrior and Rogue are your best bets. They're still built on rage and swings, and on energy and combo points. Blizzard mostly removed friction, rather than inventing new ways to play.

If you always hated the Vanilla hybrid tax, look hard at Paladin and Druid. A Prot Paladin with a taunt, and a Feral Druid whose weapon actually matters, are exactly the fixes that crowd has been asking for since two thousand and four.

If you want the single most novel Alliance experience, it's hard to beat a Dwarf Shaman.

If you always wished Survival meant the Hunter who fights up close, Forever finally does it. And with dual spec, you can keep a petless Marksmanship setup on the same character.

If you love the Priest but felt its healing trees were all kind of the same, Discipline might be your sleeper pick.

If your problem with the Mage was raid boredom, not the class itself, the new Arcane, Fire and Frost engines give you a lot more to think about.

And if you love the Warlock toolbox but wanted three real specs, Forever delivers that.

If I had to rank them purely by how different the experience feels: Shaman is top tier for novelty. Paladin is top tier for a fantasy that's finally been repaired. Druid is top tier for mechanical depth. Hunter leads the pure damage classes. Priest and Mage are excellent caster refreshes. Warlock is excellent, but more of an evolution. And Warrior and Rogue are the Classic-plus picks.

But please hear this part. That is not a power ranking. Raid damage, the tank pecking order and healing throughput are exactly the things most likely to change before launch. In the middle of October, Blizzard was still moving numbers, talents, raid buffs and cooldowns around. Picking a class based on a beta damage chart would be a mistake. Pick based on how it plays.

## Closing {#closing}

So here's the one idea I'd like you to walk away with.

Don't ask yourself which Vanilla class you already like the most. Ask which one had a fantasy you loved, but mechanics you always wished Blizzard had finished. Because that second question is exactly what Forever's class design is trying to answer.

Paladin, Druid and Shaman are the most transformed. Hunter, Priest, Mage and Warlock each gain real, distinct ways to play. And Warrior and Rogue stay closest to the Classic originals, just refined.

That's it for the Forever Class Codex. Thanks for listening, good luck picking your main, and I'll see you in Azeroth on November fourth.
